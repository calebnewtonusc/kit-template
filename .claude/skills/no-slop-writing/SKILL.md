# No slop

These rules apply to drafts, to files you create, and to your own messages to the
applicant. Not only when asked.

Two separate problems get called "AI writing." One is the machine's tics. The other is
application filler, which humans have produced without help for decades. Both make an
answer skippable. Hunt both.

## Machine tics

**No em dashes.** Colon, period, or comma. This one is nearly diagnostic on its own.

**No binary contrast.** "It's not X, it's Y." "The question isn't X, it's Y." Just
state Y.

**No throat-clearing.** "Here's the thing." "Let me be clear." "To be honest." Open on
the point.

**No faux-insight setup.** "What nobody tells you." "The part everyone misses." "Most
people get this wrong." These flatter the writer. Make the claim stand alone.

**No colon reveals.** "The best part: it learns." Write it as a sentence.

**No closing aphorism.** Delete the mic-drop line at the end. End on the last concrete
point.

**No summary endings.** "In conclusion." "Ultimately." "Overall." They just read it.

**No importance puffery.** "A testament to." "Underscores." "Speaks to." "Marks a
pivotal moment." State the fact and let the reader judge.

**No metadiscourse.** "That matters more than it sounds." "The key point is." If it is
clear, delete the aside.

**No superficial -ing clauses.** "...highlighting my commitment to collaboration."
This is the single most common tell in application essays.

**No synonym cycling.** If the right word is "org," say "org" three times.

**No rule of three by default.** "Curious, driven, and collaborative." Real writing has
uneven lists.

**No dramatic fragments.** "That's it. That's the whole thing."

**Banned words:** delve, foster, leverage, utilize, facilitate, empower, streamline,
robust, seamless, cutting-edge, transformative, elevate, harness, meticulous,
intricate, realm, tapestry, ever-evolving, myriad, testament, invaluable, pivotal.

**Cut empty phrases:** "it's worth noting," "at the end of the day," "when it comes
to," "in today's world," "the reality is," "I am eager to."

## Application filler

Different problem, same effect. These are true, positive, and worthless because
everyone writes them.

- "I am passionate about the intersection of X and Y."
- "I hope to both learn from and contribute to this community."
- "Your commitment to excellence resonates with me."
- "This experience taught me the importance of communication."
- "I thrive in fast-paced, collaborative environments."
- "I would be honored to be part of such a driven group of individuals."
- Any sentence naming a soft skill instead of showing it.

## The portability test

The one test that catches everything. **Could this sentence appear in another
applicant's essay, to another org, unchanged?**

If yes, it is filler, no matter how well written. Cut it or replace it with something
that is only true here: a name, a number, a mechanism, a specific opinion someone
could disagree with.

Run it on the opening sentence first. That is where filler does the most damage.

## Detect mode

When asked to scan rather than rewrite, report:

1. Every line with a machine tic, quoted, with the fix.
2. Every line that fails the portability test, quoted.
3. A count of banned words with locations.
4. One judgment: does this read as the applicant, or as a competent stranger.

Do not rewrite unless asked. A scan the applicant acts on themselves teaches them the
patterns. A silent rewrite does not.

## Rewrite mode

Preserve their voice. The goal is their sentences with the tics removed, not your
sentences. If they write short and blunt, keep it short and blunt. If they use a
comma splice on purpose, leave it.

Fix the pattern, change nothing else. Then show a diff-style before and after for
anything you touched, so they can veto it.
